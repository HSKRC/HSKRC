Life: from birth to death

A chronological biography based on the best available historical evidence.

1004

Birth and early environment

Nasir Khusraw was born in 1004 at Qubadiyan in the wider district of Marv, in Khurasan. Information about his immediate family is limited, but his later writings show a high level of education in Persian and Arabic literature, religious learning, philosophy and the sciences. He grew up in a region that linked Iranian, Central Asian and Islamic intellectual traditions.

Early adulthood

Administrative and court career

Before the major religious transformation of his life, Nasir Khusraw served in administrative and courtly environments associated first with the Ghaznavids and later with their Seljuq successors in Khurasan. His own poetry suggests that he knew the attractions of wealth, status and court culture from the inside. At the same time, he increasingly became dissatisfied with the outward success of that life and began searching for a more certain intellectual and spiritual foundation.

c. 1045

Spiritual crisis and decisive change

Around the age of forty or slightly later, Nasir Khusraw experienced the spiritual crisis that became the turning point of his life. In the opening of the Safarnama he relates a dream in which he was urged to seek that which would increase wisdom rather than continue a life centred on wine and worldly distraction. Modern scholarship connects this period with his conversion to Ismaili Shi'ism. Soon afterward he abandoned his administrative career and began the long journey that defined the middle period of his life.

1045–1052

Seven-year journey

His journey lasted roughly seven years. He travelled through parts of Iran and the wider Middle East, visited major cities and religious centres, performed pilgrimage, observed political and social conditions, and recorded architecture, markets, roads, water systems, religious institutions and everyday life with unusual precision. The Safarnama became one of the most important Persian travel narratives because it combines personal observation with geographical, economic and cultural detail.

Fatimid Cairo

Study in Cairo and appointment as Hujjat

A central part of his journey was his extended stay in Fatimid Cairo during the reign of the Imam-Caliph al-Mustansir. He encountered the institutions, scholarship and da'wa organisation of the Fatimid state and deepened his study of Ismaili theology and philosophy. Sources associated with the Institute of Ismaili Studies describe him as having been appointed Hujjat of Khurasan, a senior position in the Ismaili da'wa hierarchy, before his return east.

After 1052

Return to Khurasan and missionary activity

After returning from the journey, Nasir Khusraw did not resume his earlier court career. Instead he devoted himself to religious teaching, writing and the Ismaili da'wa. His activity provoked strong opposition in parts of Khurasan. Hostile religious and political pressure made ordinary public life increasingly difficult, and his poetry preserves the bitterness of persecution, isolation and displacement.

Later life

Refuge in Yumgan, Badakhshan

Eventually he found refuge in the remote valley of Yumgan in Badakhshan. This period was not simply retirement. In isolation he produced or completed many of the philosophical, theological and poetic works for which he is remembered. Badakhshan later became one of the regions where his religious and literary memory remained especially strong, and local Ismaili traditions preserved his name with exceptional reverence.

After 1070

Death and uncertainty of the exact date

The exact date of Nasir Khusraw's death is not securely established. Modern scholarly references often place his death after 1070, while a traditional approximate date of about 1077 is also widely repeated. He is generally understood to have died in or near his Badakhshan refuge. HSKRC therefore presents the date as uncertain rather than giving a false precision.

Where a date or episode is uncertain, this profile states that uncertainty rather than filling the gap with later legend.

Overview

Nasir Khusraw was one of the most important Persian-speaking Ismaili thinkers of the Fatimid period. He combined poetry, philosophy, religious teaching and travel writing, and his works later became especially influential among Ismaili communities in Iran, Afghanistan and Central Asia.

Life and journey

Born in 1004 in the wider region of Khurasan, Nasir Khusraw initially moved within courtly and administrative circles. According to the biographical tradition preserved around his own writings, a period of spiritual questioning led him to undertake a long journey that included the Hijaz and Fatimid Cairo. He spent several years in Cairo, then a major intellectual and political centre of the Fatimid world.

Ismaili role

The Institute of Ismaili Studies describes Nasir Khusraw as having converted to Ismaili Shi'ism and later being appointed hujjat for Khurasan. In Ismaili usage, this title indicated a senior position within the da'wa hierarchy. After his return east, opposition and persecution contributed to his withdrawal to Badakhshan, where he composed much of his surviving work.

Thought and intellectual contribution

His writings explore the relationship between reason, revelation, cosmology, ethics and religious interpretation. He argued that knowledge and disciplined understanding were central to the religious life, and he expressed complex philosophical ideas in both Persian prose and poetry. His work is important for studying the intellectual history of medieval Ismailism as well as Persian literature.

Major works

  • Safarnama — account of his travels.
  • Wajh-i Din — a major work on religious interpretation.
  • Gushayish wa Rahayish — theological and philosophical questions and answers.
  • Jami' al-Hikmatayn — an important philosophical work.
  • His Diwan — Persian poetry with religious, ethical and philosophical themes.

Legacy

Nasir Khusraw remains a major figure of Persian literature and Ismaili intellectual history. In Badakhshan and neighbouring regions his memory acquired enduring religious and cultural importance. Modern scholarship studies him simultaneously as a poet, philosopher, traveller and representative of the Fatimid-Ismaili intellectual tradition.
