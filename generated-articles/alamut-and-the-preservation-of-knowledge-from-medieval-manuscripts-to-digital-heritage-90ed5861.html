Source-based review · revised 30 October 2026 with AI assistance; source descriptions checked, manuscript collation and independent review not completed.

Abstract

This source-based review asks what surviving textual evidence can establish about learning at Alamut, and what a digital archive can responsibly preserve. It distinguishes a medieval work, modern descriptions of that work, and editorial recommendations. It does not reconstruct the complete contents of the lost library.

Method and limits

This revision uses the identified online scholarly entries, institutional catalogue descriptions and UNESCO charter. The facsimile manuscript and every chapter of the edited volume have not been collated for this article. Catalogue descriptions are cited as descriptions, not presented as direct examination of manuscript leaves. No independent peer review has been recorded.

The library in historical scholarship

Daftary describes Hasan Sabbah’s establishment of a library at Alamut and its later collection of manuscripts and scientific instruments. His separate Alamut entry associates the Mongol capture in 1256 with damage to the fortress and burning of the library. These are modern scholarly syntheses of a fragmentary record, not a surviving inventory. [1] [3]

A surviving witness: Dustūr al-munajjimīn

The IIS catalogue identifies this anonymous astronomical and chronological compilation as completed at Alamut in 506 AH/1113 CE. It identifies the manuscript as BnF arabe 5968 and a facsimile published in 2019. Its description reports references to books and disciplines at Alamut. Crucially, the catalogue says it is unknown whether Juwayni rescued this particular work. Its survival therefore cannot establish a specific rescue route. [2]

Transmission after the loss of a library

The description and contents of Momin’s edited volume show why textual history requires attention to copying, reading practices, authorship and community collections. Its Alamut section includes studies of newly identified manuscripts and texts. These research directions support investigating individual witnesses; they do not prove that every later Ismaili manuscript descended from Alamut. Chapter arguments require consultation of the chapters themselves. [4]

Digital preservation: an editorial application

UNESCO’s charter treats digital preservation as sustained care across a resource’s life cycle. Access must be balanced with rights and sensitive information, while authenticity requires preserving context and documentation. [5] For HSKRC, we propose linking each manuscript record to its repository and shelfmark; identifying dates, copyists and ownership only where documented; recording permissions; and retaining corrections. These are editorial recommendations, not claims that this infrastructure or a complete manuscript census already exists.

Conclusion

A documented surviving work can illuminate Alamut’s intellectual setting without restoring an entire lost library. A useful research archive should make the path from a claim to its evidence inspectable and identify what remains unknown. Future research should compare actual manuscript witnesses and critical editions before making claims about textual descent or the contents of a collection.
