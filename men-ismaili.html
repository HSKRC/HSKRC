Influential Men in Ismaili History

Biographies of men who shaped Ismaili spiritual, political, intellectual and community life across different branches and regions, from the early Imams to the present.

Historical records are uneven: many early figures have no documented childhood or authenticated portrait. Cards say when dates or personal details are uncertain. For medieval people, contextual artifacts or clearly labelled artistic images appear where available; they are not presented as verified likenesses. Nizari and Tayyibi histories are identified separately after the 1094 succession split.

Early Ismaili Imam

Isma'il ibn Ja'far

c. 719–755 CE · dates debated

Isma'il was the eldest son of the sixth Shi'i Imam, Ja'far al-Sadiq. Ismaili traditions regard him as the seventh Imam and trace their line of spiritual authority through him. Accounts differ about whether he died before his father and about the circumstances of his designation; reliable records of his childhood and private life are very limited.

Early Imam in Ismaili traditions

Muhammad ibn Isma'il

8th–9th century · dates uncertain

Muhammad was the son of Isma'il ibn Ja'far and an early Imam in Ismaili lineages. Later accounts associate his lifetime with the period of concealment, when Imams and missionaries operated under pressure from ruling authorities. His exact dates, movements and personal life remain uncertain because the surviving record is sparse.

Imam-caliph; founder of the Fatimid Caliphate

Abd Allah al-Mahdi Billah

873–934 CE · caliph from 909

Born Sa'id ibn al-Husayn in Salamiyya, he led the Ismaili da'wa from concealment and travelled west as Abbasid pressure grew. In 909, after the movement's success in Ifriqiya, he was proclaimed Imam and caliph, establishing the Fatimid state. He founded Mahdiyya in present-day Tunisia as a new capital and ruled until his death in 934. Accounts of his earliest years are shaped by the secrecy of the movement.

Fatimid Imam-caliph; founder of Cairo

Al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah

932–975 CE · ruled 953–975

Al-Mu'izz succeeded al-Mansur in 953 and transformed the Fatimid state from a North African power into a Mediterranean empire. After the conquest of Egypt in 969, his forces founded Cairo, and he moved the court there in 973. Al-Azhar became a major mosque and centre of learning during his reign. He died in Cairo in 975.

Chief judge and Ismaili jurist

Al-Qadi al-Nu'man

c. 903–974 CE

Born into a learned family in Qayrawan, al-Nu'man entered Fatimid service in 925 under al-Mahdi. He served the first four Fatimid Imam-caliphs as a judge, librarian and legal scholar, becoming chief judge in 948. Al-Mu'izz also entrusted him with teaching sessions at court. His Da'a'im al-Islam became a foundational work of Fatimid Ismaili law. He accompanied al-Mu'izz to Cairo and died there in 974.

Fatimid Imam-caliph

Al-Aziz Billah

955–996 CE · caliph from 975

Al-Aziz succeeded his father al-Mu'izz in 975 and ruled the Fatimid state from Cairo for 21 years. His reign consolidated Fatimid rule in Egypt and extended influence into Syria and the Hejaz. Sources describe a court that included Muslim, Christian and Jewish officials, though accounts of policy vary. He died in 996 and was succeeded by his son al-Hakim.

Fatimid Imam-caliph; patron of Dar al-Ilm

Al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah

985–1021 CE · ruled 996–1021

Al-Hakim became Imam-caliph as a child in 996 and ruled for a quarter-century. In 1005 he established Dar al-Ilm, or House of Knowledge, in Cairo, with a library and teaching activity. Medieval and modern accounts of his policies differ sharply. He disappeared during a night ride in 1021; his fate was never firmly established in the historical record.

Fatimid Imam-caliph

Al-Mustansir Billah

1029–1094 CE · ruled 1036–1094

Born in Cairo, al-Mustansir became Imam-caliph in 1036 while still a child and ruled for 58 years, one of the longest reigns in Fatimid history. His court presided over periods of great wealth, famine, political crisis and changing military power. When he died in 1094, a succession dispute over his sons Nizar and al-Musta'li divided the Ismaili community into Nizari and Musta'li branches.

Ismaili da'i and Sulayhid ruler of Yemen

Ali al-Sulayhi

d. 1067 CE · Sulayhid state founded c. 1047

A Yemeni Ismaili missionary, Ali al-Sulayhi built a political base in the highlands and founded the Sulayhid dynasty, which recognised the Fatimid Imam-caliphs. He expanded the da'wa and ruled much of Yemen with the support of his wife and co-ruler Asma bint Shihab. He was killed in 1067 during a tribal conflict; his son Ahmad al-Mukarram succeeded him.

Fatimid chief da'i, poet and statesman

Al-Mu'ayyad fi'l-Din al-Shirazi

c. 1000–1078 CE

Born in Shiraz, al-Mu'ayyad first served Buyid rulers while promoting the Fatimid cause in western Iran. After political danger, he travelled to Cairo, where he eventually became head of the Ismaili da'wa under al-Mustansir. He also undertook diplomatic missions in Syria and Iraq. His Arabic memoir, Sira, is a rare first-person account of Fatimid political and missionary life.

Persian Ismaili missionary and philosopher

Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani

d. c. 971 CE · birth details unknown

Al-Sijistani was a leading Ismaili da'i and philosopher in tenth-century Iran. He developed a distinctive intellectual account of creation, prophecy and religious authority, drawing on late antique philosophy while writing within the Ismaili tradition. His books, including Kitab al-Yanabi', became important sources for later Ismaili thought. Contemporary biographical records of his life are scarce.

Fatimid theologian and philosopher

Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani

fl. c. 996–1021 CE · birth date unknown

Of Persian origin, al-Kirmani flourished during the reign of al-Hakim. A senior missionary in Iraq and Iran, he was called to Cairo to address doctrinal disputes within the da'wa. He wrote major works on theology, philosophy and the Imamate, including Rahat al-'aql. Because he worked secretly in a hostile political climate, little is known about his family, childhood or final years.

Persian poet, philosopher and da'i

Nasir Khusraw

1004–1088 CE (traditional dates)

Born in Qubadiyan near Balkh, Nasir served in government before a spiritual crisis led him to travel through the Middle East. In Cairo he embraced Ismaili teaching and was appointed hujja for Khurasan. Returning east, he taught and wrote in Persian until opposition forced him into refuge in Yumgan, Badakhshan. His Safarnama records a seven-year journey; his philosophical poetry and prose shaped Ismaili communities in Central and South Asia.

Founder of the Nizari state at Alamut

Hasan-i Sabbah

c. 1050s–1124 CE

Born into a Twelver Shi'i family in Qumm, Hasan encountered Ismaili teaching as a young man and became a da'i. He studied in Isfahan and Cairo, then returned to Persia and seized Alamut in 1090. After al-Mustansir's death he supported Nizar's line and built an independent Nizari network of fortresses and communities. He died at Alamut in 1124. The familiar 'Assassin' legends about drugged followers are not supported by reliable historical evidence.

Leader of the Syrian Nizari Ismailis

Rashid al-Din Sinan

d. 1193 CE · active in Syria c. 1160s–1193

Born near Basra into a Shi'i family, Sinan grew up in Basra and became a schoolteacher before adopting Ismailism. He travelled to Alamut and studied under Hasan II, then was sent to Syria, where he became the leading Nizari da'i. From Masyaf and other strongholds he navigated conflicts with Crusader and Muslim powers. Western sources later called him the 'Old Man of the Mountain'; medieval Persian sources applied that phrase differently.

Tayyibi chief da'i and historian

Idris 'Imad al-Din

1392–1468 CE

Born in Yemen into the prominent al-Walid family, Idris became the 19th Tayyibi chief da'i and led the community during a period of political pressure. He compiled 'Uyun al-akhbar, a seven-volume history of the Ismaili Imamate from its beginnings to his own lifetime, drawing on manuscripts and reports no longer extant. His writing remains a crucial source for Fatimid and Yemeni Ismaili history.

51st al-Da'i al-Mutlaq of the Dawoodi Bohras

Syedna Taher Saifuddin

1888–1965 · led from 1915

Born in Surat, Taher Saifuddin became the 51st al-Da'i al-Mutlaq in 1915 at age 28. His long leadership strengthened the Dawat-e-Hadiyah and expanded educational, religious and community institutions. He served four terms as Chancellor of Aligarh Muslim University and wrote extensively in Arabic and Gujarati. He died in Mumbai in 1965 and was succeeded by his son Mohammed Burhanuddin.

52nd al-Da'i al-Mutlaq of the Dawoodi Bohras

Syedna Mohammed Burhanuddin

1915–2014 · led from 1965

Born in Surat, he was educated closely by his father, Syedna Taher Saifuddin, and publicly designated as successor at age 19. He assumed leadership in 1965 and guided the Dawoodi Bohra community for nearly five decades. His era saw expansion of schools, Aljamea-tus-Saifiyah, religious learning and restoration of Fatimid architecture. He died in Mumbai in 2014.

53rd al-Da'i al-Mutlaq; current Dawoodi Bohra leader

Syedna Mufaddal Saifuddin

born 1946 · current

Born in Surat in 1946, Mufaddal Saifuddin is the second son of Syedna Mohammed Burhanuddin and grandson of Syedna Taher Saifuddin. He became the 53rd al-Da'i al-Mutlaq in 2014 and leads the worldwide Dawoodi Bohra community. His public work includes community education, healthcare, environmental initiatives and humanitarian relief. He continues in office.

46th Nizari Imam; first to bear the title Aga Khan

Hasan Ali Shah (Aga Khan I)

1804–1881 CE

Hasan Ali Shah was born in Persia and succeeded his father as the 46th Nizari Imam in 1817. The Qajar monarch granted him the title Aga Khan in 1818. He served as governor of Kirman, then entered a period of conflict and political exile; after travels through Afghanistan he settled in Bombay in 1848. From India he led a transregional community reaching Persia, Central Asia and South Asia, and left memoirs that offer a rare first-person account of his life.

47th Nizari Imam

Aqa Ali Shah (Aga Khan II)

1830–1885 CE · Imam from 1881

Born in Mahallat, Persia, Aqa Ali Shah spent his early years there before studying Arabic and Nizari doctrine in Karbala. He managed Ismaili affairs in Iran and Syria for his father and succeeded him as Imam in 1881. He moved between Karachi and Bombay, served on Bombay's municipal council, developed community institutions and strengthened links with Ismailis in India, Syria and East Africa. He died in 1885 and was buried in Najaf.

48th Nizari Imam; statesman and international advocate

Sultan Mahomed Shah (Aga Khan III)

1877–1957 CE · Imam from 1885

Born in Karachi in 1877, Sultan Mahomed Shah became Imam at age seven after his father's death in 1885. He expanded community schools and institutions, advocated constitutional reform and Muslim political representation in India, and became the first president of the All-India Muslim League. He represented India at the League of Nations and served as its president in 1937. His memoirs, published in 1954, recount his public and personal life; he died in 1957.

Diplomat and Pakistan's UN representative

Prince Aly Khan

1911–1960 CE

Born in Turin, Italy, Aly Khan was the elder son of Aga Khan III. After a varied early life in Europe and South Asia, he entered diplomacy for Pakistan. In 1958 he became Pakistan's Permanent Representative to the United Nations, served as vice-president of the General Assembly and as vice-chair of the UN Peace Observation Commission. He died in a car accident near Paris in 1960.

UN High Commissioner for Refugees

Prince Sadruddin Aga Khan

1933–2003 CE · UNHCR head 1966–1977

Born in France, Sadruddin studied at Harvard and joined international service through UNESCO before working with UNHCR. He served as Deputy High Commissioner and, from 1966 to 1977, as High Commissioner—the youngest person to lead the agency at the time. His tenure responded to major refugee crises in Africa and Asia. Later he worked on UN assistance missions and environmental protection through the Bellerive Foundation. He died in Boston in 2003.

49th Nizari Imam; founder of AKDN

Prince Karim Aga Khan IV

1936–2025 CE · Imam 1957–2025

Born in Geneva in 1936, Karim spent his early childhood in Nairobi and studied at Le Rosey and Harvard, where he shifted from engineering to Islamic history. At age 20 he succeeded his grandfather as the 49th Imam in 1957. Over 67 years he developed the Aga Khan Development Network, with work in health, education, culture, architecture and economic development across many countries. He died in Lisbon on 4 February 2025 and was buried in Aswan.

50th hereditary Nizari Imam; AKDN Chair

Prince Rahim Aga Khan V

born 1971 · Imam since 4 February 2025

Born in Geneva in 1971, Rahim is the eldest son of Karim Aga Khan IV. He attended Phillips Academy Andover and graduated from Brown University in 1995 with a degree in Comparative Literature. For more than three decades he worked with his father on AKDN agency boards, with a focus on environmental protection and climate change. He succeeded his father as the 50th Imam on 4 February 2025 and now chairs AKDN.

Prince Amyn Aga Khan

born 1937 · current

Born in Geneva in 1937, Amyn is the younger brother of Aga Khan IV and was educated at Le Rosey and Harvard. Wildlife photographer and conservationist

Prince Hussain Aga Khan

born 1974 · current

Hussain Aga Khan is a son of Aga Khan IV and an internationally exhibited wildlife photographer. His work documents marine life and threatened ecosystems, using photography to encourage conservation. He founded Focused on Nature and has partnered with the Aga Khan Development Network, UNEP and museums on exhibitions and public education. Public biographies focus on his conservation work rather than private childhood details.

Historian of Ismaili studies

Farhad Daftary

born 1938 · Director Emeritus, IIS

Born in Tehran, Daftary studied in Iran, Europe and the United States and earned a PhD in economics from the University of California, Berkeley. Since joining the Institute of Ismaili Studies in 1988, he led its research department and served as co-director until 2023; he is now Director Emeritus and a governor. He has written more than 200 articles and reference entries and authored major books on Ismaili history, including The Ismailis: Their History and Doctrines and The Ismaili Imams.

Harvard scholar of Ismaili and South Asian Muslim traditions

Ali S. Asani

born and raised in Nairobi · Harvard faculty since 1983

Born and raised in Nairobi, Asani moved to the United States for Harvard College and graduated summa cum laude in 1977. He has taught at Harvard since 1983 and became a leading scholar of Ismaili and Sufi devotional traditions in South Asia. His books study ginans, Indic-language Ismaili literature, devotional poetry and religious literacy. He has served in senior academic roles at Harvard and on its Pluralism Project and Religious Literacy Project.

Karim H. Karim

Karim is a Chancellor’s Professor at Carleton University and directs its Centre for the Study of Islam. His research examines media representations of Muslims, diaspora and communication. Scholar and former Director of the Institute of Ismaili Studies

Azim Nanji

career documented; personal birth details not published

Nanji is a scholar of religion, Islam and Ismailism who served as Director of the Institute of Ismaili Studies from 1998 to 2008. He later held senior appointments at Stanford and advised the Provost at Aga Khan University. His work includes The Nizari Ismaili Tradition, Mapping Islamic Studies and The Historical Atlas of Islam. Across his career he has combined research, university leadership and public education on religious diversity.
